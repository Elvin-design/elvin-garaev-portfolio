CryptoSwiftCrypto payments for merchants who don't speak crypto

CryptoSwift lets car dealers, galleries and shops accept crypto and get paid in pounds, dollars or euros. The customer pays in BTC, ETH, USDT or USDC, and the platform locks the price, sells the crypto and sends the money to the merchant's bank. The first version worked for people who understood crypto. I redesigned the whole platform for merchants who don't, from the sale screen to the design system.

How it works

The customer pays in crypto, the merchant gets paid in pounds

A customer wants to pay for a car, a painting or a watch in crypto. The merchant enters the price in their own currency, the customer scans a code and pays from any wallet, and CryptoSwift does the rest: it checks the payment, sells the crypto at the locked price and sends the money to the merchant's bank within minutes.

CustomerPays in BTC, ETH, USDT or USDC from any wallet
Crypto
CryptoSwiftScreens the payment, locks the rate and sells the crypto
Fiat
MerchantGets GBP, USD or EUR in the bank within minutes
The customer gets the goods, and the merchant never carries the crypto risk: the price is locked at the moment of sale

01The problem

Built feature first, used by people who don't speak crypto

The platform worked, but it was built feature first and UX last. Merchants without blockchain knowledge were lost within minutes of signing up, and a sale for a car or a painting felt like a leap of faith.

1Onboarding without guidanceNew merchants were left alone with wallets, networks and settings.
2Volatility without contextNo fiat equivalent and no rate on screen while the price moved.
3Desktop onlyNo responsive layouts, though sales happen at counters and in showrooms.
4No design systemThe same element looked and behaved differently from screen to screen.
5No invoices or analyticsRemote sales and any view of the business lived outside the product.
02Research

Merchants don't want crypto. They want to get paid.

I interviewed merchants, audited the platform screen by screen and studied BitPay, CoinGate and Coinbase Commerce. The competitors had solid infrastructure and shared one weak spot: the human layer between the blockchain and the person at the counter.

Merchant interviews

With merchants, an art dealer among them, about how a crypto sale really goes.

Platform audit

Every screen and state, with more than 60 inconsistencies catalogued.

Competitive review

BitPay, CoinGate and Coinbase Commerce, compared flow by flow.

Usability tests

A simplified version against the old one, with far better comprehension.

The user the first version assumed

Crypto natives who want the details

Wallet addresses, networks and confirmations on screen, as if everyone read them.

The user we actually had

Merchants who think in pounds

They wanted three answers: how much, did it work, and when the money arrives.

“
I had no idea if it worked. The screen said something. I didn't know if I should wait or do something else.
Art dealer, merchant interview, 2023

Merchants care about getting paid and having a paper trail, not about the blockchain.

That became the north star for every screen: speak in fiat, show one status anyone can read and keep a record of every sale. The blockchain stays in the background.

03Fiat first

The price in pounds never moves

Crypto prices move every second, and a merchant shouldn't have to watch them. The price is entered in fiat and locked for the sale, and the crypto amount became a secondary line that updates itself.

Fiat is primary and crypto is secondary on every screen, from the new sale to the receipt.

04Status first

One status anyone can read

Behind every sale there are five technical steps: the AML check, the crypto arriving, the transfer to the exchange, the sale and the payout. Merchants see them as one line of progress under a single status.

The most visible thing on the screen is a green Completed when the money is on its way, and the sales history keeps the paper trail merchants asked for.

06Trust and compliance

Compliance that doesn't feel like paperwork

Accepting crypto comes with rules. Every payment is screened, every merchant is verified and no address is ever used twice. The design job was to keep all of it visible and none of it in the way.

AML check on every payment

Incoming payments are screened with Elliptic before a sale moves on. Merchants see it as the first step of the sale, not as a risk report.

Verified merchants

Every business passes KYC through an identity verification partner before its first sale.

A new address for every sale

Single use wallets mean a payment can never land on the wrong sale.

Own blockchain nodes

Payments are tracked on CryptoSwift's own nodes instead of a third party service.

Penetration testing

The platform is tested by independent security specialists.

Money in the bank within minutes

Funds move to the merchant's bank account automatically, with no manual withdrawal.

10Reflection

What I learned

Designing for crypto taught me that the best interface for a complex technology hides it. Merchants never needed to understand a blockchain. They needed a number they could trust and a clear yes.

Fiat first and status first did more for confidence than any explanation could. When the price stopped moving and the status turned green, the questions stopped too.

If I started again, I would invest in analytics from day one. Merchants soon asked for conversion by coin and for their peak sale hours, and those insights would have helped them sell more.
